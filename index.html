What is Instruct-FD?

Can a full-duplex speech system change its turn-taking behaviour when instructed to?

Existing full-duplex benchmarks — Full-Duplex-Bench v1 [1], v1.5 [2], FLEXI [3] — test whether a model can produce a fixed turn-taking behaviour at a cue, for instance taking the turn soon after the user finishes a question.

The limitation is that a model needs to adjust its turn-taking behaviour based on the conversation and the deployment context:

Interrupt me if you hear me making a mistake.
Let me finish my thoughts before jumping in.

One scenario, two opposite instructions

Every turn-taking instruction specifies which floor action to take at which cue. Instruct-FD pairs each scenario with two instructions that demand different actions at the same cue, so a model only scores by responding to the instruction rather than to the moment.

Scenario-controlled contrastive evaluation

A model's default behaviour can masquerade as instruction following — an eager model takes the floor because it always does, not because it was told to. So each case fixes one scenario and runs it twice under contrasting instructions. The audio is identical; only the instruction changes.

Figure 1. Overview of the Instruct-FD benchmark taxonomy. Each case shows the contrastive instruction pair with example good model responses.

Five cue categories

Temporal
Silence and timing — a mid-utterance pause, or how long an overlap lasts.
Linguistic
What is said — a factual error, or a keyword.
Paralinguistic
How it is said — hesitation, a whisper or a sigh.
Sound events
Non-speech audio — a doorbell, a phone ringing.
Speaker attribution
Who is speaking — a second voice joining, or a remark addressed to someone else.

Four contrastive instruction pairs

T/S  Take turn vs Silent T/B  Take turn vs Backchannel B/S  Backchannel vs Silent Y/C  Yield vs Continue
Scoring

Passing means obeying both

Paired pass rate is the headline metric. A case counts as passed only when the model produces the prescribed behaviour under both instructions. Getting one side right and the other wrong scores zero, which is what separates genuine instruction following from a default that happens to match one of the two.

Per-instruction pass rate scores each condition on its own, ignoring its partner. It is the looser measure, and the gap between the two is informative: a system can score well per-instruction simply by always staying silent, and still score near zero paired.

What the systems are actually doing

Per-instruction pass rates reveal each system's default turn-taking behaviour.

Figure 4. Per-instruction pass rate (%) by instructed behaviour, each condition scored on its own. Closed-source APIs above the rule.
  • Closed-source APIs default to holding back. Silent 71–86% and yield 58–75%, but take turn ≤ 15%.
  • Open-weight defaults differ. MiniCPM-o 4.5 and DuplexCascade keep talking (continue 93% / 74%); RL-tuned models backchannel (Moshi-RL 82%, PersonaPlex-RL 60%).
  • No system follows take-turn instructions more than 24% of the time. Speaking into the user's floor, on cue, is the capability nobody has.

Systems act on floor availability, not the instruction: more silence at the cue means more taking, less staying silent.

Figure 5. Pass rate (%) against the floor left open at the cue. Pooled over the 15 system configurations, ρ = +0.23 for take and −0.26 for silent.

GPT-Live-1's lead comes from following backchannel-vs-silent instructions and from temporal cues.

Figure 6. (a) GPT-Live-1 pass rate by cue type and instruction (1,358 sessions). (b) GPT-Live-1 paired pass rate by cue type and instruction contrast. It adjusts best on temporal cues and worst on sound events. Take turn succeeds only on temporal cues (47% vs ≤ 15%), so proactive behaviour that requires barging into the user's floor is still weak. Y/C is low because it tends to always yield.

Delivering the instruction as text does not help, and turn-taking fluency does not predict instructed turn-taking.

Figure 7. (a) Open-weight systems move ≤ 0.9 points with a text instruction; GPT-Live-1 drops 3.7 points. (b) Instruct-FD pass rate against FDB scores reported by Artificial Analysis [5] shows no correlation, ρ = −0.07 (n = 7).

Interaction RL post-training improves backchannelling, but hurts overall turn-taking instruction following.

Figure 8. Pass rate (%) before and after interaction post-training [4]. RL improves pass rate on backchannel cases substantially (+34.2 and +36.4 points) but lowers the B/S paired pass rate — the gain comes from backchannelling more often, including when told to stay silent.
